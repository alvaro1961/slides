The Adjoint of a Convolution

Introduction to Machine Learning

Alvaro Arias

Inner Product Spaces

An inner product space is a vector space \(V\) equipped with an inner product \(\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle : V \times V \to \mathbb{R}\): bilinear, symmetric, and positive definite.

  • \(\mathbb{R}^n\)
  • \(M_{n\times m}\)
  • An RGB Image
  • A CNN Layer with \(C\) Channels

The usual dot product:

\[\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i\]

The Frobenius inner product on matrices:

\[\langle A, B\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij} B_{ij} = \operatorname{tr}(A^\top B)\]

An RGB image is a tensor \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{p\times q\times 3}\), indexed by row, column, and color channel. The inner product is the natural extension of the Frobenius one:

\[\langle X, Y\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^p \sum_{j=1}^q \sum_{c=1}^3 X_{i,j,c}\, Y_{i,j,c}\]

Equivalently, summing the Frobenius inner product of each color channel separately:

\[\langle X, Y\rangle = \sum_{c=1}^3 \langle X_c, Y_c\rangle\]

A convolutional layer’s feature map is a tensor \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{p\times q\times C}\), one value per spatial position per channel. RGB is the special case \(C=3\); the inner product extends the same way:

\[\langle X, Y\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^p \sum_{j=1}^q \sum_{c=1}^C X_{i,j,c}\, Y_{i,j,c} = \sum_{c=1}^C \langle X_c, Y_c\rangle\]

Everything that follows only needs an inner product to be defined — the same argument works whether the objects being compared are vectors, matrices, or images.

The Adjoint of a Linear Map

Let \(V, W\) be inner product spaces and \(T : V \to W\) a linear map. The adjoint of \(T\) is the linear map \(T^* : W \to V\) defined by:

\[\langle Tv, w\rangle_W = \langle v, T^*w\rangle_V \quad \text{for all } v \in V,\ w \in W\]

Special case. If \(T : \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n\) is linear, there exists and \(n\times m\) matrix \(A\) such that \(T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}\).

Then, using the standard dot product on both sides, we easily check that \(T^*\) is multiplication by \(A^\top\), the transpose of \(A\).

In machine learning, adjoints are usually just called transposes.

Convolution as a Linear Map

Let \(K\) be a \(k\times k\) kernel and \(X\) an \(N\times N\) image. Cross-correlation gives a linear map

\[C : M_N \to M_M, \qquad M = N - k + 1\]

\[C(X)_{i,j} = \sum_{a=0}^{k-1}\sum_{b=0}^{k-1} K_{a,b}\, X_{i+a,\,j+b}\quad\text{ for }\quad 1\leq i,j\leq M\]

The question is how do we find \(C^*\).

To find the answer, fix \(Y\in M_M\) and \(X\in M_N\). Then look at \(\langle C(X), Y\rangle\), perform algebraic operations and obtain \(\langle C(X), Y\rangle=\langle X, \text{RHS}\rangle\)

Since \(X\) is arbitrary, \(\text{RHS}=C^*(Y)\)

Computing the Adjoint

Start expanding the left-hand side

\[\langle C(X), Y\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^M\sum_{j=1}^M Y_{i,j} \sum_{a=0}^{k-1}\sum_{b=0}^{k-1} K_{a,b}\, X_{i+a,\,j+b}\]

Rearrange the sum. For each entry of \(X\), collect every term where it appears, and substitute \(m = i+a,\ n=j+b\):

\[\langle C(X), Y\rangle = \sum_{m=1}^N\sum_{n=1}^N X_{m,n} \sum_{a=0}^{\min(m-1,k-1)} \sum_{b=0}^{\min(n-1,k-1)} K_{a,b}\, Y_{m-a,\,n-b}\]

Since this holds for arbitrary \(X\), for \(1\leq m,n\leq N\), \[C^*(Y)_{m,n} = \sum_{a=0}^{\min(m-1,k-1)} \sum_{b=0}^{\min(n-1,k-1)} K_{a,b}\, Y_{m-a,\,n-b},\] which looks similar to the convolution formula.

The Adjoint Is Also a Convolution

It is not hard to see that the last formula is the cross-correlation with the kernel flipped, applied to a padded \(Y\).

For \(0\leq i,j<k\), let \(\tilde K_{i,j} = K_{k-1-i,\,k-1-j}\) (the flip of \(K\)), and let \(Y_p\) be \(Y\) padded with a frame of \(p = k-1\) zeros. Then we have that:

\[C^*(Y) = C_{\tilde K}(Y_p)\]

Therefore the adjoint of a convolution is another convolution with a flipped kernel and padded input.

Convolution with Stride

A strided convolution \(C_{K,s}\) of kernel \(K\) with stride \(s\) factors as an ordinary convolution followed by subsampling: \(C_{K,s} = S_s \circ C_{K,1}\) where \(S_s\) keeps every \(s\)-th entry.

Adjoints reverse composition order: \(C_{K,s}^* = C_{K,1}^* \circ S_s^*\)

  • Example, the Map \(S_2\)
  • The adjoint of \(S_2\)

(stride-2 subsampling):

\[ S_2 : \begin{pmatrix} X_{11} & X_{12} & X_{13} & X_{14} & X_{15} \\ X_{21} & X_{22} & X_{23} & X_{24} & X_{25} \\ X_{31} & X_{32} & X_{33} & X_{34} & X_{35} \\ X_{41} & X_{42} & X_{43} & X_{44} & X_{45} \\ X_{51} & X_{52} & X_{53} & X_{54} & X_{55} \end{pmatrix} \;\longmapsto\; \begin{pmatrix} X_{11} & X_{13} & X_{15} \\ X_{31} & X_{33} & X_{35} \\ X_{51} & X_{53} & X_{55} \end{pmatrix} \]

\(S_2\) keeps every other row and every other column, discarding the rest.

\[ S_2^* : \begin{pmatrix} Y_{11} & Y_{12} & Y_{13} \\ Y_{21} & Y_{22} & Y_{23} \\ Y_{31} & Y_{32} & Y_{33} \end{pmatrix} \;\longmapsto\; \begin{pmatrix} Y_{11} & 0 & Y_{12} & 0 & Y_{13} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ Y_{21} & 0 & Y_{22} & 0 & Y_{23} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ Y_{31} & 0 & Y_{32} & 0 & Y_{33} \end{pmatrix} \]

\(S_2^*\) places each entry of \(Y\) back where it came from, and fills every discarded position with a zero — the adjoint of “throw entries away” is “put zeros where they were thrown away,” never any other value.

Convolution with Dilation

A dilated convolution \(C_{K,d}\) of kernel \(K\) with dilation \(d\) can be written as a regular convolution with a different kernel.

It is not hard to see that \[C_{k,d}=C_{S_d^*(K)}\] where the new kernel is the adjoint of the stride-\(d\) operator.

Then \[C_{K,d}^*=C_{S_d^*(K)}^*.\]

Adjoint in PyTorch

The adjoint of the convolutions operators are implemented in PyTorch

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

conv = nn.Conv2d(1, 1, kernel_size=3, stride=2, padding=1, bias=False)
transposed_conv = nn.ConvTranspose2d(1, 1, kernel_size=3, stride=2, padding=1, bias=False)

x = torch.randn(1, 1, 9, 9)
y = conv(x)
print("input: ", x.shape)
print("output:", y.shape)
print("transposed_conv(output):", transposed_conv(y).shape)
input:  torch.Size([1, 1, 9, 9])
output: torch.Size([1, 1, 5, 5])
transposed_conv(output): torch.Size([1, 1, 9, 9])

nn.ConvTranspose2d (or F.conv_transpose2d) takes the same padding, stride, and dilation arguments as Conv2d and builds \(C^*\) directly — the flipping, padding, and zero-insertion from the last three slides are handled internally.

Checking the Definition Numerically

The defining identity, \(\langle Cx, y\rangle = \langle x, C^*y\rangle\), holds exactly — not approximately — when both layers share the same kernel:

torch.manual_seed(0)
conv = nn.Conv2d(1, 1, kernel_size=3, stride=2, padding=1, bias=False)
transposed_conv = nn.ConvTranspose2d(1, 1, kernel_size=3, stride=2, padding=1, bias=False)
transposed_conv.weight.data = conv.weight.data.clone()

x = torch.randn(1, 1, 9, 9)
y = torch.randn_like(conv(x))

lhs = torch.sum(conv(x) * y)
rhs = torch.sum(x * transposed_conv(y))
print(f"<Cx, y>   = {lhs.item():.6f}")
print(f"<x, C*y>  = {rhs.item():.6f}")
<Cx, y>   = 0.884554
<x, C*y>  = 0.884554

Uses of the Adjoint

Two places this operator shows up, beyond being mathematically interesting.

  • Backpropagation — computing gradients through a convolutional layer.
    • This happens behind the scenes in Pytorch.
    • Because the adjoint of a convolution is a convolution, backprop reuses the same optimized convolution routines instead of ever forming the matrix \(A\) explicitly.
  • Increasing spatial dimension — going from a compressed representation back to larger resolution.
    • Suppose that \(C\) is a convolution of kernel size \(k=2\), stride = 2, and no padding (padding = 0).
    • This map reduces the spatial dimension in half \(C:M_{2n}\to M_n\).
    • Then the adjoint doubles the dimension \(C^*:M_n\to M_{2n}\).
    • These maps are a natural building block for bottleneck architectures like U-Net, which are widely used in computer vision and diffusion models.
